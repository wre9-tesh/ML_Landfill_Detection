What this manual lets you do

Starting from the label files in the repository, you rebuild version 3 of the project: the sample points, the satellite features, the nine trained models, the frozen model, the Gujarat score maps and the diagnostic analysis. At each step you can check your output against the copy stored in the repository.

It does not cover collecting the labels: finding landfills, drawing their outlines in Google Earth and screening the OpenStreetMap candidates. Those files are given. docs/phase1_labels.md describes how they were made.

What you need to know already

How to open a terminal and type a command, and what a folder and a CSV file are. Nothing about Git, Earth Engine, rasters or scikit-learn is assumed; section C installs all of them. The machine-learning terms (precision, recall, fold) are explained in the plain-language guide, docs/project_guide_plain.pdf.

Two ways to use the repository's stored files

Every intermediate file except the Gujarat rasters is stored in the repository. That gives you a choice at each step.

  • Rebuild and compare. Run the step, then compare your output with the stored copy. This is what the manual describes.
  • Skip. Use the stored copy and move on. Steps 4, 5, 8, 9 and 10 need an Earth Engine account or several hours. A reader without them can still do steps 2, 3, 6, 7 and 11.1 and reproduce every validation number.

BBackground: why version 3 exists

The project asks whether a model trained on Indian landfills outside Gujarat can find Gujarat's landfills from free satellite data. Nothing from Gujarat is used in training. A list of 17 outlined Gujarat dumpsites was sealed in its own commit before any model was run, and each model is frozen under a Git tag before Gujarat is scored with it.

Version 1Version 2Version 3
What changedThe original plan: 12 features, 9,530 points25,800 more background points from around the training landfills14 more features: a winter season and each pixel's surroundings
Model selectedLogistic regressionRandom forestGradient boosting
Gujarat sites found, of 17161516
Land flagged5.5%1.8%5.3%
Known sites among the top 100 alarm patchesnot recorded48
What the top alarms were0 of 50 checked were dumps: mostly blue roofs and waterLarge bare-land patchesBeing checked
  • Version 1 failed as a detector. A linear model gave extreme scores to blue metal roofs, which were absent from its training data.
  • Version 2 removed the roofs by adding background points from towns and coasts, but then ranked large bare fields first.
  • Version 3 adds what a person uses to tell a field from a dump: how the place looks in another season, and what surrounds it. It is the version this manual rebuilds.
  • The project is written up as a diagnostic study, because even version 3 flags about 5% of the land. The three versions and a hand check of their alarms are the evidence.

Version 3 uses version 2's training points and version 1's twelve features, so rebuilding it rebuilds most of the earlier versions' inputs too. The full results of versions 1 and 2 are in data/analysis/depth_analysis.md, docs/freeze.md and docs/freeze_v2.md.

What a rerun of Gujarat means

Version 1 is the only first-time test. Versions 2 and 3 were designed after earlier Gujarat results were seen, and the same 17 sites have been scored three times. Rebuilding version 3 reproduces a reported result; it is not a new blind test.

Inside the script

  1. Read the decisions and outlines. Kept OpenStreetMap rows and hand-drawn outlines become landfill places. Dropped rows with a look-alike reason become look-alike places.
  2. Merge neighbours. Outlines within 500 m of each other are one place. This stops one landfill from sitting on both sides of a validation split.
  3. Drop small places. A hand-drawn place under 1 ha gives no points. One hectare is about one pixel of the temperature satellite.
  4. Draw points inside each place. Up to 50 per landfill place and 30 per look-alike place, each at least 15 m inside the outline and 15 m from the next point.
  5. Draw random background. As many random points as landfill points (3,865), shared between states in proportion to their landfill points, never within 500 m of any mapped landfill or look-alike, never in Gujarat.
  6. Assign states and regions. Each point gets the state it falls in. The script also reports which states have enough places to be a validation fold of their own. Only Maharashtra does, so validation uses the six Zonal Council regions.
SettingValueWhy
--n-site, --n-lookalike50, 30One 300 ha landfill must not outweigh thirty 3 ha ones

Inside the script

  1. Find each landfill place from the site points of step 2, and take the rectangle that reaches 15 km beyond them in every direction. This is how the Gujarat boxes are drawn in step 8.
  2. Draw 300 random points in each rectangle. 86 places times 300 is 25,800.
  3. Reject a draw that lies within 500 m of any mapped landfill, hand-drawn site or look-alike, or that falls in Gujarat, in Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, or in the sea.

Each point is labelled 0 with kind near_site. The seed is es678-v2, one random stream per place.

Inside the script

  1. Read the points and check the cache. A point already fetched with the same settings is skipped. The settings are hashed, so changing any of them refetches everything.
  2. Sort the remaining points by state and position, so each batch of 300 covers a small area and needs few satellite scenes.
  3. Build the feature image. For the area a batch covers, Earth Engine is told how to compute the twelve layers: filter the scenes by date and cloud, mask bad pixels, take the median, compute the indexes. Nothing is calculated yet; this is a recipe.
  4. Read the image at the points. Earth Engine evaluates the recipe only at the batch's points, at 10 m, and returns twelve numbers each.
  5. Handle failures. A rate limit makes the program wait and retry. Any other failure halves the batch and retries each half, down to 20 points.
  6. Write the outputs. Each batch is appended to cache.csv at once. At the end, features.csv is the points table with the twelve columns added, and features_meta.json records every setting used.

A point whose pixel is masked in every scene comes back empty. In the stored tables that is 58 of 9,530 points (snow in the Himalaya) and 66 of 25,800; training leaves them out.

Inside the script

  1. Build one table. The two base tables are stacked (35,330 rows). The ten extra columns are joined to them by point_id, and the four differences are computed. Rows with any missing value are dropped, leaving 34,345: 3,853 landfill and 30,492 other.
  2. Make the folds. A fold is a part of the data hidden during training and used for testing. Three kinds are made:
    • Held-out region (the headline): six folds, one per Zonal Council region. All points of a region are hidden together.
    • Held-out site: five folds; all points of a place stay together.
    • Random pixel: five folds of randomly chosen points. This is the flattering split, because neighbouring pixels of the same landfill are in training.
  3. Train and test. For each split, each model and each fold: fit the model on the other folds, score the hidden fold, flag every point scoring 0.5 or more, and compute precision, recall and F1.
  4. Average. The mean and standard deviation across folds go into summary.csv.
  5. Select. The model with the best mean F1 on the held-out-region split is selected (--rule best).
  6. Record. selection.json holds the selected model, the folds, the feature list and the tables used. Step 7 reads it.

The nine models

All use scikit-learn's default settings except where stated, weights that make landfill and non-landfill points count equally, a threshold of 0.5 and the random seed 678. Nothing is tuned.

Inside the script

  1. Read selection.json to learn which model was selected and which tables it was trained on.
  2. Rebuild the same 34,345-row table as step 6.
  3. Fit the selected model on every row.
  4. Save it with joblib, and write model.json: the model's name, its 26 features in order, the threshold, the differences to compute, and the scikit-learn version.

What "frozen" means in this project

In the original run, the commit holding this model was given the Git tag freeze-v3 and the tag was pushed to GitHub before Gujarat was scored. The tag's time stamp is the evidence that the model, threshold and scoring rules were fixed first. The rules themselves are in docs/freeze_v3.md.

The scoring program in step 9 enforces this. It runs only if the model file is the one under the tag. You do not need to create a tag: your clone already carries freeze-v3.

8Export the Gujarat rasters

Training read features at 35,000 points. Scoring Gujarat needs them at every pixel of fourteen rectangles, about 134 million pixels. Earth Engine computes these and writes them to your Google Drive as GeoTIFF files, which you then download.

Definitions

A raster is a grid of pixels with one or more numbers per pixel. A GeoTIFF is a raster file that also records where on Earth the grid sits. A band is one layer of numbers in it; here, one feature.

Why the boxes come from a file

Each box reaches 15 km beyond the sealed sites of one city, so it contains towns, farmland, industry and coast in their real proportions, not only the land around a dump. labels/gujarat_boxes.json stores the boxes exactly as they were scored. Two of them cannot be recomputed from the current site list: the Surat box was drawn when the list still held a pin that was later removed, and Surendranagar is a fourteenth box around one landfill that is in neither the training data nor the sealed list.

The 13 city boxes cover 11,914 km², about 6% of Gujarat. The rest of the state is not scored.

Inside the script

  1. Read the boxes.
  2. For each box, build the same feature image as in steps 4 and 5, using the very same function. Training points and Gujarat pixels therefore cannot be computed differently.
  3. Ask Earth Engine to export it: 10 m pixels, in the map projection UTM zone 43N (EPSG:32643), as a GeoTIFF with one band per feature, named after the feature.
  4. Record the boxes, settings and task numbers in export_meta.json.

Inside the script

  1. Check the freeze (step 7).
  2. Pair the files. For each gujarat_<city>.tif, open gujaratx_<city>.tif and confirm the two grids are the same size, position and projection.
  3. Score. Read 512 rows at a time. Compute the four differences. A pixel with all 26 values gets the model's score; any other pixel is marked missing. The score map is saved as whole numbers from 0 to 100, with 255 for missing.
  4. Count each pixel once. Boxes of neighbouring cities overlap. Files are processed in alphabetical order, and a pixel already covered by an earlier box is not counted again.
  5. Flag. A pixel scoring 0.5 or more is flagged.
  6. Judge each sealed site. The outline is laid over the grid. The site is found if at least half of its scored pixels are flagged.
  7. Find the alarms. Flagged pixels outside the sealed outlines that touch along an edge are grouped into patches. Patches of at least 1 ha (100 pixels) are alarms. Each gets its centre, area and mean score.
  8. Rank. Alarms are sorted by mean score, highest first.
  9. See where the known sites would rank. The grouping is repeated with the sealed sites left in. site_ranks.csv says where each site's patch stands among all patches.
  10. Prepare the hand check. The top 100 alarms get shuffled numbers A001 to A100 and are written as pins to alarms_blind.kml.

10Check the top alarms by eye

How many of the strongest alarms are real dumps is the number a user of the map cares about, and no program can produce it. A person looks at each place.

Runs onYou, in Google Earth Pro
TimeAbout a minute per pin; 100 pins
Writesblind_check.csv in the results folder
  1. Open rep\gujarat_results_v3\alarms_blind.kml in Google Earth Pro. A hundred pins appear, named A001 to A100. The names are shuffled, so a pin's number says nothing about its rank or score.
  2. For each pin, use the historical-imagery slider to find imagery from 2023 to 2025.
  3. In a spreadsheet with the columns blind_id, verdict, class, notes, seen, record one row per pin. The sheet used in the project is data/gujarat_results_v3/blind_check.csv.
ColumnWhat to write
verdictdumpsite if accumulated waste of about 1 ha or more is visible; lookalike if not; unsure only when the imagery cannot settle it
classFor a look-alike: construction, bare_soil, industrial, riverbed, ash_pond, quarry or other
seenWhat is actually there, in a word or two: blue_roof, water, field_or_grass, salt_pan, building

Why the scores are hidden. Knowing that a pin is the model's top pick makes it easier to see a dump there.

This step cannot be reproduced exactly, because it is a human judgement. Two careful people will agree on most pins and differ on a few.

Inside the script

  1. One feature at a time. For each of the 26 features and each kind of background, compute the AUC: the chance that a random landfill point has a higher value than a random background point. 0.5 means the feature tells them apart no better than a coin.
  2. All features together, with no model. Put every feature on the same scale. For each point, find its 15 nearest points that belong to other places, and record what share of them are landfill.
  3. A picture. Principal components squeeze the 26 features into two axes holding as much of the variation as possible, and the points are drawn on them.
  4. What each version still confuses. From the held-out-region scores of the three frozen models: the share of each kind of point flagged.
  5. What each model relies on. SHAP values measure how much each feature pushed each score up or down. They are summed over groups of related features, because related features share credit arbitrarily.

Inside the script

  1. Take the centres of the top 100 alarms of each of the three versions and of the 17 sealed sites: 317 places.
  2. Read the 26 features at each, as the median of the 3 by 3 pixels around the centre.
  3. Novelty. Measure each centre's distance to its 15 nearest training points and express it as a percentile of the training points' own such distances. Above the 99th percentile, the model is being asked about something unlike its training data.
  4. Nearest class. Record what the 15 nearest training points are.
  5. SHAP per alarm. For the version 3 model, record which feature groups pushed each centre's score up.
  6. Join the hand-check verdicts where they exist and summarise by kind of alarm.

As of 15 November this program had been tested on made-up rasters only; its results on the real rasters were not yet in the repository.

What a rerun cannot establish

  • That the labels are right. They were judged by one person from imagery.
  • That the freeze came first. The evidence for that is the time stamps of the tags freeze-v1, freeze-v2 and freeze-v3 and of the seal commit 8711855 on GitHub.
  • How the model does elsewhere in Gujarat. Only the boxes were scored.
