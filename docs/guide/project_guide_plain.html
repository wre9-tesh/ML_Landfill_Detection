1The project in one minute

Cities in India send their rubbish to landfills and open dumpsites. Many are poorly recorded, and some are not recorded at all. Satellites photograph every part of the country every few days, for free. This project asks: can a computer program look at satellite data and point out where the dumpsites are?

The program is shown examples of known dumpsites across India, everywhere except the state of Gujarat. It works out what dumpsites have in common. Then it is tested, once, on Gujarat, a state it has never seen. If it finds Gujarat's known dumpsites, it has learned something real. If it does not, that is also an honest answer.

The project is as much about testing honestly as about building the program. A large part of it shows how easy it is to make such a program look far better than it is.

2Why this is worth doing

  • Dumpsites cause harm. They catch fire, pollute groundwater and release methane, a gas that warms the climate.
  • Records are incomplete. Official lists miss informal dumps, and maps go out of date as sites grow, close or move.
  • Checking on foot is slow. A method that points inspectors to likely places would save time, even if it is not always right.
  • Published methods may be over-optimistic. Some studies report accuracy above 90%, but they often test the program on the same places it learned from. This project checks what happens under a stricter test.

3How a computer learns from examples

Definition: machine learning

A way of getting a computer to do a task by showing it many examples with the right answers, instead of writing out the rules by hand. The computer finds patterns in the examples and uses them on new cases.

Think of teaching a child to recognise mangoes. You do not give a written rule. You show many fruits and say "mango" or "not a mango" each time. After enough examples the child can judge a fruit never seen before. Machine learning works the same way, with five ingredients.

IngredientMeaningIn this project
ExamplesThe cases the computer learns fromSmall squares of ground, each 10 m × 10 m
LabelsThe right answer for each example"Landfill" or "not a landfill", decided by a person looking at imagery
FeaturesThe measurements that describe each exampleTwelve numbers per square: colour, temperature, height and so on
ModelThe pattern-finder: a procedure that turns the features into an answerNine different ones are compared
TestExamples kept hidden during learning, used to check the modelRegions held back in turn, and finally Gujarat
Definitions: training and testing

Training is the learning stage: the model sees examples together with their labels. Testing is the checking stage: the model sees new examples without labels, gives its answers, and those answers are compared with the truth. A model must be tested on examples it did not train on, for the same reason an exam should not reuse the practice questions.

4What a satellite sees

Definition: pixel

A satellite image is a grid of tiny squares called pixels. In this project one pixel covers 10 m × 10 m of ground, about the floor area of a small house. For each pixel the satellite records a few numbers.

A phone camera records three colours: red, green and blue. Satellites record more.

  • Visible light. Blue, green and red, as the eye sees them.
  • Infrared light. Light the eye cannot see. Healthy plants reflect a great deal of near-infrared light, and dry bare ground reflects a great deal of shortwave-infrared light. These make it easy to tell plants, soil and buildings apart.
  • Heat. Some satellites measure how hot the ground surface is.
  • Height. A radar survey flown on the Space Shuttle measured the height of the land everywhere.

Each of these measurements is called a band. Three sources are used:

SourceWho runs itWhat it gives
Sentinel-2European Space AgencyVisible and infrared light, in 10 m to 20 m pixels, every 5 days
Landsat 8 and 9NASA and the US Geological SurveyGround surface temperature, in pixels of about 100 m
SRTMNASAHeight of the land above sea level

All three are free. They are read through Google Earth Engine, an online service that stores satellite data and does the heavy calculation on Google's computers.

Why a landfill should look different

  • Almost no plants grow on active waste.
  • The surface is mixed and disturbed: plastic, soil, ash and rubble.
  • It is warm. Rotting waste gives off heat, and a dark, dry surface heats up in the sun.

The difficulty is that other places share these traits: quarries, bare fields, construction sites, ash ponds at power stations. In this project these are called look-alikes, and they matter a great deal.

6Step 1: collecting examples

Definition: label

The right answer attached to an example. Here a label says whether a piece of ground is a landfill. Labels are made by a person, and the model can only be as good as its labels.

Where the landfill examples came from

A public map. OpenStreetMap is a free world map that volunteers draw, a little like Wikipedia for maps. It has 917 places in India marked as landfill. Of these, 13 were removed at once (marked as closed, or really quarries), 47 inside Gujarat were set aside, and 857 remained as candidates.

Checking by eye. Volunteers make mistakes, so each candidate was opened in Google Earth, a program that shows detailed aerial photographs, including older ones. The photograph closest to March–May 2024 was used, because that is when the satellite measurements were taken. Each place was marked keep, drop (with the reason) or unsure.

The result was sobering. Of 107 places checked, only 13 were real, active landfills. Sixty were look-alikes: 32 ash ponds, 16 patches of bare soil and 12 quarries. The rest were closed, in the wrong position or could not be judged.

Drawing our own. Because the public map gave so few, the student outlined 82 well-known municipal dumpsites by hand in Google Earth, in cities from Delhi and Mumbai to Guwahati and Chennai. After tidying up, this gave 75 places.

Examples for trainingPlaces
Landfills from the public map11
Landfills outlined by hand75
Landfills in total86
Look-alikes (ash ponds, bare soil, quarries)60

Rules for a fair set of examples

  • The waste must be visible in 2024. A site that closed in 2022 would teach the model the wrong thing, because the satellite measured it in 2024.
  • At least 1 hectare of waste. A hectare is 100 m × 100 m, about one and a half football pitches. The temperature satellite cannot see anything smaller, so smaller sites are left out. Three hand-drawn sites fell below this.
  • Nearby outlines count as one place. Two outlines within 500 m of each other are treated as one landfill. Four pairs were merged this way.
  • Nothing from Gujarat. Not one Gujarat example is used in training.

Why look-alikes are collected on purpose

If the model only ever compared landfills with forests and fields, it would learn "bare, warm ground means landfill" and flag every quarry in the country. Showing it quarries, bare soil and ash ponds labelled "not a landfill" forces it to learn finer differences.

The Gujarat answer sheet

For the final exam, the student listed and outlined known dumpsites in Gujarat: 17 sites in 13 cities, including Pirana in Ahmedabad and Khajod in Surat. This list was saved and time-stamped before any model was run on Gujarat. One further site has only a pin and no outline, so it cannot be marked.

Why seal the list

If the list could be edited after seeing the model's map, it would be tempting to add places the model found and forget places it missed. Sealing it first makes that impossible.

7Step 2: choosing points

The model learns from individual pixels, not from whole outlines. A program picked 9,530 points:

Kind of pointHow manyLabel
Inside landfills3,865Landfill
Inside look-alikes1,800Not a landfill
Random places in the same states3,865Not a landfill

The choices behind this, and the reasons:

  • At most 50 points per landfill. Otherwise one giant landfill would dominate, and the model would learn "what Deonar in Mumbai looks like" instead of "what landfills look like".
  • Points stay 15 m inside the outline. Pixels on the edge are part waste and part something else, which would blur the lesson.
  • Random points stay 500 m away from any mapped landfill. This avoids labelling a real dump as "not a landfill" by accident.
  • As many random points as landfill points. The model sees a balanced mix. This is convenient for learning, but it is not what the real world looks like; section 12 returns to this.

8Step 3: twelve measurements

Definition: feature

A measurement that describes an example. If you were judging fruit, features might be weight, colour and firmness. Here each point has twelve features taken from satellite data.

All twelve describe the ground in March to May 2024. These are the dry months before the monsoon, when the sky is clear and grass has died back, so waste is not hidden under greenery.

FeatureIn plain wordsWhat it helps tell apart
Blue, green, red (3 features)The ordinary colour of the groundDark waste, pale soil, green crops
Near infraredLight just beyond red; plants reflect it stronglyVegetation from everything else
Shortwave infraredLight further beyond red; dry and built surfaces reflect itDry soil, concrete and waste from wet or green ground
Vegetation index (NDVI)One number for "how green and alive"Low on waste, high on crops and forest
Built-up index (NDBI)One number for "how built-up or disturbed"High on towns, rubble and waste
Bare soil index (BSI)One number for "how bare"Bare ground from roofs and roads
Surface temperature (LST)How hot the ground is, in °CLandfills run hot
Temperature anomalyHow much hotter the spot is than the land within 2 kmA warm spot in a cool area, whatever the local climate
ElevationHeight above sea levelPlains from hills
SlopeHow steep the ground isFlat ground from quarry faces and hillsides
Definition: index

A single number made by combining two or more bands so that one property stands out. The vegetation index, for example, compares near-infrared light with red light. Plants reflect much near-infrared and little red, so the index is high over plants and low over bare ground.

Why the temperature anomaly matters. 40 °C is hot for Shillong and ordinary for Rajasthan. A rule based on raw temperature would fail when moved between climates. "4 °C hotter than its neighbourhood" means the same thing everywhere, so it should carry over to a new state.

Why a three-month median. For each pixel the middle value of all clear images in the three months is taken. A cloud, a shadow or a single odd day then has no effect.

What the measurements already show. On average, landfill points are 4.7 °C hotter than their surroundings. Look-alikes and random points are not hotter at all. The vegetation index, by contrast, is the same for landfills and look-alikes. So greenness alone cannot separate a dump from a quarry, but heat can help.

9Step 4: nine ways to decide

Definition: model

A procedure that takes the features of an example and gives an answer. Training is the process of adjusting the model until its answers match the labels on the training examples. In this project a model gives each pixel a score between 0 and 1; a pixel scoring 0.5 or more is flagged as a likely landfill.

Nine models are compared, from trivially simple to quite elaborate. Comparing them shows whether complexity earns its place.

ModelHow it decides, in plain words
1. Flag nothingSays "not a landfill" every time. A deliberate zero, to compare the others against
2. Published ruleThree fixed tests from an earlier study in Madhya Pradesh: built-up index above a limit, vegetation index below a limit, temperature above 37 °C. All three must pass
3. Recalibrated ruleThe same three tests, but the limits are re-chosen from our own training examples
4. Logistic regressionGives each feature a weight, positive or negative, adds everything up, and turns the total into a score. Like a marking scheme: so many marks for heat, so many taken off for greenness
5. Decision treeA chain of yes/no questions, like the game of twenty questions: "Hotter than 44 °C? Yes. Vegetation index below 0.2? …"
6. Bagged trees200 trees, each trained on a slightly different mix of the examples, voting together. A committee is steadier than one member
7. Random forest500 trees voting, each also restricted to a random few features at each question, so the trees differ more from one another
8. AdaBoost200 very simple one-question trees built in sequence; each pays most attention to the examples the earlier ones got wrong
9. Gradient boosting100 small trees built in sequence, each correcting the errors that remain after the ones before it
Definitions: bagging and boosting

Both combine many simple models into one stronger one, called an ensemble. In bagging the models are built independently and vote, which steadies the answer. In boosting they are built one after another, each concentrating on the mistakes left by the earlier ones.

No fine-tuning. Each model has settings that could be adjusted to squeeze out a slightly better mark. This project deliberately uses standard settings. With so few landfills, adjustments would mostly chase luck, and the comparison would become a contest of effort instead of method.

10Step 5: grading fairly

Three kinds of mock exam

The same models were graded in three ways, from easiest to hardest.

Mock examWhat is hidden during trainingThe catch
Random pixelA random fifth of all pointsNeighbouring pixels from the same landfill are still in the training set. It is like an exam that repeats the practice questions with one word changed
Held-out siteWhole landfills, a fifth at a timeThe model has seen other sites in the same region, with similar soil and climate
Held-out regionA whole region of IndiaThe hardest and fairest: nothing from that region was seen. This is the closest rehearsal for Gujarat
Definitions: cross-validation and fold

Cross-validation means repeating the train-and-test cycle several times, hiding a different part of the data each time, so that every example is used for testing exactly once. Each hidden part is called a fold. The marks from all the folds are averaged.

India was divided into the six official Zonal Council regions: Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern and North-Eastern. In each round one region is hidden, the model trains on the other five, and it is marked on the hidden one. The division was written down before any results were seen.

Three marks

Suppose the model flags 100 pixels, and 80 of them really are landfill. Suppose also that there were 90 landfill pixels in total.

Definitions: precision, recall, F1

Precision answers "when the model raises an alarm, how often is it right?" Here 80 of 100 alarms are right: 80%.

Recall answers "of the real landfills, how many did the model find?" Here it found 80 of 90: 89%.

F1 is a single mark that combines the two and is high only when both are high. Here it is about 84%.

Both matter. A model that flags everything finds every landfill (perfect recall) but is nearly always wrong (terrible precision). A model that flags one certain pixel is always right (perfect precision) but misses nearly everything. A wrong alarm is called a false alarm or false positive.

The rule for choosing the winner

Written down on 4 November, before any results:

  1. Find the model with the best average F1 in the held-out-region exam.
  2. Look at how much its mark varies from region to region (its standard deviation).
  3. If a simpler model is within that margin of the best, choose the simplest such model.
Definition: standard deviation

A measure of spread: roughly, how far the individual marks typically sit from their average. If a model scores 0.90 in one region and 0.80 in another, a difference of 0.02 between two models is too small to mean anything.

Why prefer the simpler model. If two models cannot be told apart by their marks, the simpler one is easier to explain, easier to check, and less likely to be relying on a fluke.

This is one of the project's main findings already. On the easy exam the tree committees look excellent: 95%. On the hard exam they drop by 11 or 12 points and are no better than plain logistic regression, which barely moves.

Definition: overfitting

Learning the training examples too closely, including their quirks, instead of the general pattern. It is like a student who memorises the answers to the practice paper. That student does brilliantly if the exam repeats the practice questions and poorly if it does not. The big trees memorised individual landfills; the easy exam rewarded that, and the hard exam exposed it.

A study that reported only the easy exam would have announced 95% and picked the wrong model.

What the chosen model still gets wrong

In the hard exam, logistic regression wrongly flags:

  • 5 in every 100 ordinary background points;
  • 20 in every 100 ash-pond points;
  • 39 in every 100 bare-soil points;
  • 43 in every 100 quarry points.

Quarries and bare soil remain hard. The North-Eastern region, with only five landfills, gives the shakiest marks.

12The rarity problem

This is the least intuitive idea in the project, and the most important one to be able to explain.

In the mock exams about 4 points in 10 are landfill, because the points were chosen that way. In the real world landfills are rare: far fewer than 1 pixel in 100. That changes everything about how often an alarm is right.

Definition: prevalence

How common the thing you are looking for is. If 1 pixel in 1,000 is landfill, the prevalence is 0.1%.

A worked example

The chosen model finds 89 of every 100 landfill pixels and wrongly flags about 5 of every 100 other pixels. Take one million pixels of real countryside (100 square kilometres) and suppose 1 pixel in 1,000 is landfill.

PixelsFlagged by the model
Landfill1,000890 (true alarms)
Everything else999,000about 50,930 (false alarms)
All flagged pixelsabout 51,820

Only 890 of about 51,820 alarms are real: less than 2%. The model has not become worse. There is simply so much "everything else" that even a small error rate produces a flood of false alarms.

If landfill pixels areShare of alarms that are real
4 in 10 (the mock exam)83%

1 in 1,0001.7%
1 in 10,0000.2%

The same effect appears in medical screening: a good test for a rare disease still gives mostly false positives, because healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones.

What follows from this
  • The model's output is called a score, never a probability or a confidence. A score of 0.9 does not mean "90% likely to be a landfill".
  • The Gujarat exam covers every pixel of large areas, not a hand-picked balanced sample, so the rarity is real.
  • The useful way to use such a model is as a shortlist: look first at the highest-scoring places, and check them by eye.

13Step 6: sealing the envelope

Definition: the freeze

The moment when every choice is fixed: which examples, which features, which model, what score counts as an alarm, and how success will be judged. All of it is saved in the project's online code store with a permanent, time-stamped marker called a tag. After the freeze nothing may change.

The time stamp comes from GitHub, the website that stores the code, so anyone can verify that the choices were fixed before Gujarat was examined.

Before the freeze, four things still need settling:

  1. Whether to add more examples. Allowed now; forbidden afterwards.
  2. When a Gujarat landfill counts as "found". The first idea was "at least one flagged pixel inside its outline". That is far too generous: because 5% of ordinary pixels are flagged anyway, almost any outline would contain one by luck. The recommended rule is that at least half of the outline must be flagged.
  3. What to do about one site that has no outline.
  4. What would count as failure. One sentence, written by the student before the result is known.
Why define failure in advance

After the result is known, almost any number can be described as encouraging. A sentence written beforehand, such as "finding fewer than half the sites is a failure", keeps the conclusion honest.

14Step 7: the final exam in Gujarat

The exam paper

For each of the 13 Gujarat cities with a listed dumpsite, a square of land is taken that reaches 15 km beyond the sites in every direction. Each square is roughly 30 km × 30 km and holds about 9 million pixels. Together the 13 squares hold about 125 million pixels, covering some 12,500 square kilometres.

For every one of those pixels the same twelve measurements are produced, exactly as they were for the training points. These files are being prepared now. Preparing them reveals nothing about the result, so it is safe to do before the freeze.

Marking the exam

After the freeze, the model gives every pixel a score. Three things are then counted.

  1. How many of the 17 listed sites were found. This is recall at the level of whole sites.
  2. How much land was flagged in total. This shows how many alarms someone would have to deal with.
  3. How good the top alarms are. Flagged pixels that touch are grouped into patches. Patches of at least 1 hectare are ranked by score, and the top 100 are checked by eye.
Definition: blind check

The 100 top places are given to the student as numbered pins in shuffled order, with the scores hidden. The student looks at each in Google Earth and records "dumpsite", "look-alike" or "unclear" without knowing how highly the model ranked it. This prevents the natural tendency to see what one expects to see.

Some of those top alarms may be real dumpsites that are missing from the list. Finding those would be the most practically useful outcome.

The one rule of the final exam

Whatever the Gujarat numbers are, they are the result. If they are poor, the paper says so and examines why. Nothing is adjusted and the exam is not retaken.

15Step 8: digging deeper

The course asks for more than a headline number. Five further analyses explain why the result is what it is. None of them is allowed to change the frozen model.

Definition: ablation

An experiment in which one part of a method is removed, or one condition changed, and everything is measured again. The difference shows how much that part mattered.

AnalysisThe question it answers
Split ablationHow much does the mark fall as the exam gets fairer: random pixel, held-out site, held-out region, Gujarat?
Feature ablationWhich measurements matter? The model is retrained without temperature, without terrain, with only the published rule's three inputs, and so on
Precision against prevalenceHow often is an alarm right as landfills become rarer? (Section 12)
Threshold sensitivityWhat would have happened with a stricter or looser cut-off than 0.5? Reported for interest only
Failure auditWhat kinds of places fool the model, and in which regions?

16Step 9: writing it up

  • A four-page paper in the course's format: the question, the method, the results, the limitations.
  • A website that replaces an oral exam. It will show a map of Gujarat with the model's scores, the listed sites marked found or missed, the charts from the deeper analysis, a slider showing how precision falls with rarity, and a gallery of look-alikes that fooled the model.
  • The code and data, organised so that another person can download them and reproduce every number.

Honest weaknesses

  • Few examples. 86 landfills is a small number. Marks for regions with few sites are unreliable.
  • Mostly big, famous dumps. The hand-drawn examples are the ones that were easy to find. Small informal dumps may look different.
  • Only three kinds of look-alike. Brick kilns, construction sites and dry riverbeds were not collected, so the model has never been told they are not landfills.
  • A short answer sheet. With 17 Gujarat sites, missing a single one changes the result by 6 points.
  • Blurry temperature. The temperature satellite sees 100 m blocks, so a small site's heat is mixed with its surroundings.

18How to explain this project to someone else

In one sentence

We taught a computer to recognise rubbish dumps in satellite data using examples from the rest of India, and we are testing it, once and under sealed conditions, on a state it has never seen.

In two minutes

  1. The goal. Rubbish dumps are harmful and poorly mapped. Free satellite data covers the whole country. We want a program that points to likely dumps.
  2. The method. We marked 86 known dumps and 60 look-alike places across India, outside Gujarat. For thousands of points inside and outside them we collected twelve satellite measurements: colour, plant cover, bareness, temperature and terrain. A program learned what separates dumps from everything else.
  3. The honest test. We hid one region of India at a time and checked whether the program could find dumps there. We compared nine programs and chose with a rule fixed in advance. The simple one, logistic regression, was chosen at 85%.
  4. The first lesson. When tested the easy way, on pixels from the same dumps it trained on, the fancier programs scored 95%. Tested on unseen regions they fell to about 84%. Easy tests flatter.
  5. The second lesson. Dumps are rare. Even a program that is right 9 times in 10 on a balanced test will raise mostly false alarms on a real map, because there is so much land that is not a dump. The output is a shortlist for people to check, not a verdict.
  6. The final exam. All choices are sealed with a public time stamp. Then the program scores 125 million pixels around 13 Gujarat cities, and we count how many of 17 known dumps it finds and how good its top 100 alarms are.

Three numbers worth remembering

NumberWhat it means
85%The chosen model's F1 mark on regions it never saw
95% → 84%How far the tree committees fall between the easy exam and the fair one
83% → under 2%How the share of correct alarms falls when dumps go from 4 in 10 to 1 in 1,000

19Questions people ask

Why not just use the model with the highest mark?

Its lead over the simpler model is smaller than its own variation between regions, so the lead may be luck. A simpler model that performs the same is easier to explain and check.

Why leave Gujarat out of training? Surely more examples would help.

They would, but then there would be nowhere left to test honestly. The point of the project is to find out whether the method works in a place it has not seen, which is how it would be used in practice.

Is 85% good?

It is good on a balanced mock exam. It does not mean 85% of alarms on a real map will be right; section 12 shows why that share will be far lower. Both statements are true at once.

Why can't I upload a photo of a place and ask the model?

The model does not look at pictures. It reads twelve numbers per pixel, including infrared light and temperature, which an ordinary photo or screenshot does not contain.

Why only March to May 2024?

The examples were checked against imagery from that period, so the measurements must come from the same period. It is also the driest, clearest season, when waste is least hidden by plants or cloud.

What if the Gujarat result is bad?

Then that is the finding. The project is designed so that a poor result is still trustworthy and useful, because it shows how far published accuracy figures can overstate real performance.

Could the model find dumps nobody knows about?

Possibly. The blind check of the top 100 alarms is where that would show up. Any such find would need confirming on the ground.

Who did what?

The student chose the question, marked every example by eye and makes every decision. An AI assistant (Claude) helped plan the study, wrote the programs and keeps the records. The paper will state this, as the course requires.

20Glossary

An experiment that removes one part of a method, or changes one condition, to see how much it mattered.
Alarm
A place the model flags as a likely landfill.
Ash pond
A basin where a coal power station stores wet ash. A common landfill look-alike.
Band
One kind of measurement a satellite records for each pixel, such as red light or near-infrared light.

Building many models on slightly different mixes of the examples and letting them vote.
Blind check

Boosting
Building many simple models one after another, each concentrating on earlier mistakes.
Cross-validation
Repeating training and testing several times, hiding a different part of the data each time.
Decision tree
A model that decides through a chain of yes/no questions.
Ensemble
A model made by combining many simpler models.
F1
A single mark that combines precision and recall; high only when both are high.
False alarm (false positive)
A place flagged as a landfill that is not one.
Feature
A measurement that describes an example. This project uses twelve per pixel.
Flagged
Given a score of 0.5 or more, and so marked as a likely landfill.
Fold
One of the parts into which data is divided for cross-validation.
Freeze
The moment all choices are fixed and publicly time-stamped, before the final test.
Google Earth Engine
An online service that stores satellite data and processes it on request.
Hectare (ha)
An area of 100 m × 100 m, or 10,000 square metres.
Held-out
Kept hidden from the model during training so that it can be used for testing.
Index
One number made from two or more bands so that a single property stands out, such as the vegetation index.
Label
The right answer attached to an example: landfill or not a landfill.
Landsat
A series of US satellites. Here it supplies ground surface temperature.
Logistic regression
A model that gives each feature a weight, adds them up and turns the total into a score.
Look-alike
A place that resembles a landfill from above but is not one, such as a quarry.
Median
The middle value of a set of numbers. Unlike the average, it is not thrown off by a few odd values.
Model
A procedure that turns the features of an example into an answer.
OpenStreetMap (OSM)
A free map of the world drawn by volunteers.
Overfitting
Learning the training examples too closely, quirks included, and so doing worse on new cases.
Pixel
One square of a satellite image. Here, 10 m × 10 m of ground.
Precision
Of the places the model flags, the share that really are landfills.
Prevalence
How common the thing being looked for is.
Recall
Of the real landfills, the share the model flags.
Score
The number between 0 and 1 the model gives a pixel. Higher means more landfill-like. It is not a probability.
Sentinel-2
A pair of European satellites that record visible and infrared light.
Standard deviation
A measure of how much a set of numbers varies around its average.
Tag
A permanent, time-stamped marker on a saved version of the project's code and data.
Threshold
The cut-off score at or above which a pixel is flagged. Here, 0.5.
Training
The stage in which a model learns from examples that come with labels.
Zonal Council regions
Six official groupings of India's states: Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern and North-Eastern.
